Why Crash Diets Fail (and What to Do Instead)

The scale drops fast, then the weight returns. Here is why extreme diets rarely last, and what works better.

JG Janani Gokul Sandar 4 Oct 2026 5 min read

Almost everyone has tried one: a plan that cuts out entire food groups, or has you eating a tiny amount for a couple of weeks. The first few days feel exciting because the number on the scale falls quickly. Then real life gets in the way, and the weight creeps back. That is not a personal failure. It is what these plans are designed to do.

Why crash diets backfire

1. Early weight loss is mostly water

When you suddenly eat far less, especially fewer carbohydrates, your body releases stored water along with a little fat. The quick drop is encouraging, but much of it is not fat, and it comes back as soon as you eat normally again.

2. You can lose muscle along with fat

Very low intakes, combined with too little protein and no strength training, make it easier to lose muscle. Muscle helps you burn energy and keeps you strong, so losing it works against you in the long run.

3. Hunger and cravings catch up with you

Eating far too little makes you hungry, tired and preoccupied with food. Willpower rarely wins that fight for long. Many people end up in a cycle of strict restriction followed by overeating, which leaves them feeling worse than when they started.

4. The plan doesn't fit your life

A diet built around special products, rigid rules or skipping family meals is almost impossible to follow at weddings, on busy weeks or when you are travelling. If a plan only works when life is perfect, it won't work for long.

A note on safety Very low calorie diets can cause fatigue, dizziness and nutrient shortfalls, and they are not suitable for everyone. If you are considering one, speak to your doctor first.

What works better

Lasting weight loss usually comes from small changes that you repeat for months. Here is the approach I use with my clients:

  1. Use a modest deficit. Eating a little below your maintenance calories is enough. Try the calorie calculator to find your starting point.
  2. Keep the foods you enjoy. Learn portions that work, rather than banning favourites. A plan with room for your favourite meals is a plan you can stick with.
  3. Build around protein, fibre and plenty of vegetables. These keep you fuller on fewer calories.
  4. Move every day. Start with a daily walk, then add strength training when you are ready.
  5. Change one habit at a time. Master one small step, such as drinking more water or adding a vegetable to lunch, before adding the next.
  6. Expect imperfect weeks. A slip-up is information, not failure. Adjust and carry on.

Measure more than the scale

Your weight fluctuates daily, so judge progress over several weeks. Also notice energy levels, sleep, strength, how your clothes fit and how you feel about food. Those signs often improve well before the scale shows a big change.

The goal isn't speed A steady loss of around 0.25–0.5 kg (about ½–1 lb) per week is a healthy, sustainable pace for most people. It may feel slow, but it is the version that stays off.

This article is for general information and is not medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant or take medication, please speak to your doctor before changing your diet or exercise routine.
